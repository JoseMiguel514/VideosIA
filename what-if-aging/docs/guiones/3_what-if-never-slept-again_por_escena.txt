[s01]
Imagine you go to bed tonight... and nothing happens. No drifting. No dreams. Your eyes stay open until morning. And the next night. And the next. You will never sleep again.

[s02]
At first, it sounds like a superpower. Eight extra hours every single day. More time to work, to play, to live. But your brain has a very different opinion. Let's watch what happens, hour by hour.

[s02b]
Think about it. If you live to around eighty, you'll spend something like twenty-five years of your life asleep. A third of everything. Surely evolution wouldn't waste that much time unless it really, really mattered.

[s03]
Hour sixteen. This is a normal day. You woke up at seven, it's eleven at night, and you feel a little tired. Totally fine. Your body is building up a pressure to sleep, like air filling a balloon.

[s04]
That pressure comes partly from a chemical called adenosine. It slowly builds up in your brain the longer you're awake. Coffee works by blocking it. For a while.

[s04b]
Your body also runs on an internal clock, roughly twenty-four hours long. As it gets dark, your brain releases a hormone called melatonin, a signal that says, it's night, time to wind down.

[s04c]
And caffeine lingers. For many people, about half of a cup's caffeine is still in their body five or six hours later. That afternoon coffee can still be working at bedtime.

[s05]
Hour seventeen. You keep going. And something sneaky happens. A study in Australia found that after seventeen to nineteen hours awake, people performed some tasks as badly, or worse, than someone at a blood alcohol level of point zero five percent.

[s06]
In many countries, that's close to the legal limit for driving. You haven't had a single drink. But your brain is already acting a little drunk.

[s06b]
That's why drowsy driving is treated as a serious danger by road safety agencies around the world. Tired drivers react slower, drift between lanes, and sometimes don't brake at all.

[s07]
Hour twenty-four. One full day. The sun comes up and you feel a strange second wind. That's your internal clock, your circadian rhythm, telling your body it's morning. It's lying to you.

[s08]
Your reaction time is slower. Your mood is shorter. You read the same sentence three times. Your emotions get louder, because the part of your brain that keeps them in check is the part getting tired first.

[s08b]
At work, you nod along in a meeting, but nothing sticks. Attention is one of the first things to fail. Not all at once, but in tiny lapses you don't even notice.

[s09]
And here's something that happens at night that you never see. While you sleep, your brain seems to rinse itself. Studies in mice found that sleep helps flush away waste products between brain cells, including a protein linked to Alzheimer's disease.

[s10]
Tonight, that cleaning crew never shows up. The trash just keeps piling up.

[s10b]
Your hunger starts acting strange, too. Studies suggest that short sleep can shift the hormones that control appetite. Many people feel hungrier, especially for sugary, salty, and fatty food.

[s11]
Hour thirty-six. Now it gets weird. You're sitting at your desk, staring at a screen, and suddenly, you lose a few seconds. You didn't decide to sleep. Your brain just took it. That's called a microsleep.

[s12]
A microsleep can last from a fraction of a second to several seconds. Your eyes might even stay open. You won't remember it. And if you're driving at highway speed, a few seconds is the length of a football field.

[s12b]
That's one reason pilots, truck drivers, and many other workers have strict limits on how long they can work without rest. Not because they're weak, but because no human brain is immune to this.

[s13]
This is the cruel twist. You can try to never sleep. But your brain will start stealing sleep anyway, in tiny pieces, whenever it can. You are no longer fully in control.

[s14]
Hour forty-eight. Two days. Your body is struggling too. Even short-term sleep loss can change how you handle sugar. In one classic study, healthy young men who slept just four hours a night for six nights processed glucose noticeably worse.

[s15]
Your immune system is taking hits as well. In another study, people who usually slept less than six hours were about four times more likely to catch a cold after being exposed to the virus.

[s15b]
Doctors see the long-term version of this. Regularly sleeping too little is linked with higher risks of obesity, diabetes, high blood pressure, and heart disease.

[s16]
And your memory? Sleep is when your brain files away what you learned today. Skip it, and new information starts slipping through the cracks, like writing on a whiteboard that nobody saves.

[s16b]
You try another coffee. It helps, for an hour. Then the crash hits harder. Caffeine can block the sleepy signal, but it can't remove the pressure that keeps building underneath.

[s17]
Hour seventy-two. Three days. Many people who stay awake this long start to see things that aren't there. Shadows moving at the corner of the eye. Patterns crawling on the walls. Voices that might be real... or might not.

[s17b]
Even ordinary places start to feel wrong. The lights hum too loudly. Faces look strange. Colors seem too bright, and the floor feels like it's gently tilting.

[s18]
Your speech slurs. Your thoughts slip and loop. Simple math becomes hard. You might feel paranoid, like everyone is watching you. Your brain is running on fumes, and it knows it.

[s18b]
Your body starts to feel off, too. You're cold, then hot. Your hands might tremble. Your eyelids feel heavy, as if they're made of sand. Every blink is a negotiation.

[s19]
So how long can a human actually stay awake? In nineteen sixty-four, a seventeen-year-old student named Randy Gardner tried to find out, for a high school science fair.

[s20]
He stayed awake for two hundred and sixty-four hours. Eleven days. Friends kept him moving. Playing basketball. Playing pinball. Anything to keep his eyes open.

[s20b]
A Stanford sleep researcher named William Dement heard about the attempt and came to watch. He helped keep Randy safe, and took notes on what the sleepless brain was doing.

[s21]
By the end, he had trouble with memory, concentration, and his mood. Then he slept for about fourteen hours, and seemed to recover. But decades later, he said he struggled with insomnia, and wondered whether the stunt played a part.

[s22]
Guinness World Records eventually stopped accepting attempts at this record, because of the health risks. Think about that. Even the people who measure the most extreme things on Earth said, please, don't do this one.

[s22b]
There have been other claims of people staying awake far longer. But most were never carefully monitored. And some may have included microsleeps that nobody noticed, not even the person themselves.

[s23]
But Randy slept in the end. What happens if you truly can't? Scientists have studied this in animals. In experiments in the nineteen eighties, rats that were kept from sleeping completely died within a few weeks.

[s24]
Their bodies seemed to fall apart from the inside. They ate more but lost weight. They struggled to keep warm. Scientists still debate exactly what killed them. But the message was clear. Sleep is not optional.

[s24b]
Of course, a rat is not a person. And experiments like that would never be allowed on humans. So for people, we have to look somewhere else. And what we find is chilling.

[s25]
And in humans, there's a real disease that comes terrifyingly close to our What If. It's called fatal familial insomnia.

[s26]
It's an extremely rare genetic condition. A misfolded protein slowly damages the thalamus, a part of the brain that helps control sleep. Over time, patients lose the ability to sleep properly.

[s27]
It usually starts in middle age with insomnia, then spreads to the rest of the body. Racing heart. Sweating. Problems with movement and thinking. There is no cure yet, and it is fatal, often within a year or two.

[s28]
It's one of the strongest pieces of evidence we have. A human brain that cannot sleep, in the end, cannot survive.

[s28b]
Some animals have found clever workarounds. Dolphins can rest one half of their brain at a time, keeping the other half alert enough to breathe and watch for danger.

[s28c]
Scientists have even recorded frigate birds sleeping while flying, in short bursts of just seconds. Nature never found a way to skip sleep. Only clever ways to squeeze it in.

[s28d]
Some seals do something similar. In the water, fur seals can sleep with one half of the brain, while the other half stays awake.

[s29]
So, back to you. Day five. You've stopped trusting your own eyes. Day seven. Your microsleeps are now constant. You might be sitting with your eyes open, technically awake, but barely there.

[s30]
And this is the strangest part. In real life, you almost certainly wouldn't make it this far. Not because you'd die, but because your brain would win. At some point, sleep would simply take you, wherever you are.

[s30b]
Severely sleep deprived people can nod off sitting up, standing in line, or in the middle of a sentence. The brain doesn't ask permission.

[s31]
That's the real answer to this What If. You can't actually choose to never sleep. Sleep isn't a habit. It's a need, like breathing. Hold your breath long enough, and your body will breathe for you.

[s32]
Which raises a better question. If sleep is that important, why do so many of us treat it like the first thing to cut? Most adults need around seven or more hours a night. Many of us get less.

[s32b]
New parents know a gentle version of this. In the first months after a baby arrives, nights are broken into pieces, and many parents feel like they're thinking through fog.

[s33]
Every all-nighter, every late scroll on your phone, is a small version of this experiment. Not deadly. But not free, either. Your brain always collects the debt.

[s33c]
Screens play a part too. Bright light late at night can delay melatonin, nudging your internal clock later, so sleep arrives later than it should.

[s33b]
The good news is that the basics are simple. Go to bed and wake up at similar times. Keep your room dark and cool. Get some daylight in the morning. Your clock loves routine.

[s34c]
Moving your body helps as well. Regular exercise during the day is linked with falling asleep faster and sleeping more deeply at night.

[s34]
So here's your challenge. Tonight, put the phone down a little earlier. Let the cleaning crew do its job. Your future self will think more clearly, feel calmer, and remember more of today.

[s34b]
Because in the end, the What If has a simple answer. A life without sleep isn't a life with more time. It's a life where every hour gets a little blurrier, until the brain takes back what it's owed.
